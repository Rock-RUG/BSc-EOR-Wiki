Interpretation¶

A point mass is a single value that carries positive probability on its own. On the CDF it shows up as a jump: just to the left of aaa the CDF approaches FX(a−)F_X(a^-)FX​(a−), at aaa it equals FX(a)F_X(a)FX​(a), and the height of the step is exactly P(X=a)\mathbb{P}(X=a)P(X=a).

Because FXF_XFX​ is right-continuous, the mass at aaa is included in FX(a)=P(X≤a)F_X(a)=\mathbb{P}(X\le a)FX​(a)=P(X≤a), while the left limit FX(a−)F_X(a^-)FX​(a−) equals P(X<a)\mathbb{P}(X<a)P(X<a). With atoms present, a strict and a non-strict inequality can therefore give different probabilities.

Every value with positive PMF of a discrete random variable is a point mass, and these masses add up to one. A point mass can also be only one part of a distribution: in the Example, 0.30.30.3 sits at zero and the remaining 0.70.70.7 is spread by a density, a mixture of a discrete and a continuous part. An atom at aaa therefore does not mean that all probability sits at aaa.

A continuous random variable has a CDF without jumps, so P(X=a)=0\mathbb{P}(X=a)=0P(X=a)=0 for every aaa, even where the density is large: density height is not point probability.

This differs from a constant random variable, for which the point mass at its only possible value is one. An absolutely continuous distribution has no point masses.
