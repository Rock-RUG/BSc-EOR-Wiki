Weierstrass Approximation Theorem¶

Theorem¶

For a continuous function f ⁣:[a,b]→Rf\colon [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}f:[a,b]→R and any ε>0,\varepsilon > 0\text{,}ε>0, there exists a polynomial P ⁣:[a,b]→RP\colon [a, b]\to\mathbb{R}P:[a,b]→R such that ∥P−f∥∞<ε.\|P-f\|_\infty < \varepsilon\text{.}∥P−f∥∞​<ε.

Interpretation¶

Any continuous function on a closed bounded interval can be approximated as closely as desired by a polynomial, uniformly on the whole interval: for each tolerance ε\varepsilonε there is a polynomial whose graph stays within ε\varepsilonε of the graph of fff at every point of [a,b][a,b][a,b].

The errors shrink as the degree grows, which is what the theorem promises.

Both hypotheses are needed. A polynomial is continuous, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so a function with a jump cannot be approximated uniformly by polynomials. The interval must be closed and bounded as well: every polynomial is bounded on (0,1](0,1](0,1], so none stays within ε\varepsilonε of the unbounded function 1/x1/x1/x there, and on R\mathbb{R}R even ex\mathrm{e}^xex cannot be approximated, as in the Examples.

The approximating polynomials are not partial sums of one power series. The sum of a power series is differentiable inside its interval of convergence, so ∣x∣|x|∣x∣ is not such a sum around 000, yet it is a uniform limit of polynomials; in the figure even the constant term changes from one pnp_npn​ to the next. The theorem guarantees existence only; Bernstein polynomials, used in the interactive scene, are one explicit construction. It differs from the Taylor polynomials met later, which are built from derivatives at a single point.
