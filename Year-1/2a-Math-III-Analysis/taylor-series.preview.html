Interpretation¶

If a function is so smooth that it behaves exactly like an infinite polynomial near a point, then that polynomial is built entirely from its derivatives at that point. The Taylor series makes this precise: for a real analytic fff, knowing f(a),f′(a),f′′(a),…f(a),f'(a),f''(a),\dotsf(a),f′(a),f′′(a),… determines fff on a whole interval around aaa.

Real analyticity is the hypothesis that makes the series equal fff. Being smooth is enough to write the series down, but not for it to converge to fff, as the smooth function in the Key Results shows. Even for an analytic function the equality is limited by the interval of convergence: ln⁡(1+x)\ln(1+x)ln(1+x) is defined for all x>−1x>-1x>−1, but its Taylor series at 000 converges only for −1<x≤1-1<x\le1−1<x≤1.

Taylor series are how exponentials and trigonometric functions are handled as sums. Key Results¶

  • The Taylor series centred at the origin is called the Maclaurin series.
  • For any smooth function you can write down its Taylor series around aaa, but it need not converge to the function: it equals fff on a neighbourhood of aaa if and only if fff is real analytic at a.a\text{.}a. For example, f(x)=e−1/x2f(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-1/x^2}f(x)=e−1/x2 for x≠0x\neq0x=0, f(0)=0f(0)=0f(0)=0, is smooth, but its Taylor series at 000 is identically 0.0\text{.}0.
