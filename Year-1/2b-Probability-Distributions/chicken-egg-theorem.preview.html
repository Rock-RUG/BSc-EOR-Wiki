Chicken–Egg Theorem¶

Theorem¶

Let λ>0\lambda>0λ>0, 0<p<10<p<10<p<1 and q=1−pq=1-pq=1−p. If N∼Pois⁡(λ)N\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)N∼Pois(λ) and, given N=nN=nN=n, X∼Bin⁡(n,p)X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)X∼Bin(n,p) for every n≥0n\ge0n≥0, then X∼Pois⁡(λp)X\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda p)X∼Pois(λp) and N−X∼Pois⁡(λq)N-X\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda q)N−X∼Pois(λq), and XXX and N−XN-XN−X are independent.

The Poisson Distribution supplies the random total NNN. Given that total, the Conditional Probability Mass Function of XXX is a Binomial Distribution PMF: independently labelling nnn items produces a binomial count before averaging over NNN.

Interpretation¶

A Poisson total divided by binomial labelling produces two independent Poisson counts, even though their sum is the original total. The name comes from the picture in the figure: a Poisson number of eggs is laid, and each egg hatches independently with probability ppp.

In the sample shown, ten eggs are laid; three hatch (red) and seven do not (blue). Over repeated samples the hatched count is Pois⁡(λp)\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda p)Pois(λp), the unhatched count is Pois⁡(λq)\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda q)Pois(λq), and the two counts are independent: for instance, λ=10\lambda=10λ=10 and p=0.3p=0.3p=0.3 give Pois⁡(3)\operatorname{Pois}(3)Pois(3) and Pois⁡(7)\operatorname{Pois}(7)Pois(7).

The surprise is the independence. For a fixed total n≥1n\ge1n≥1 the counts are dependent, since they must add up to nnn: many hatched eggs force few unhatched ones. Given N=0N=0N=0, both counts are constant zero and are independent. With a Poisson total this dependence disappears, because the joint PMF factorizes:

P(X=k, N−X=m)=e−λp(λp)kk!⋅e−λq(λq)mm!.\mathbb{P}(X=k,\,N-X=m) =\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda p}\frac{(\lambda p)^k}{k!}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda q}\frac{(\lambda q)^m}{m!}.P(X=k,N−X=m)=e−λpk!(λp)k​⋅e−λqm!(λq)m​.

This follows from P(N=k+m)(k+mk)pkqm\mathbb{P}(N=k+m)\binom{k+m}{k}p^kq^mP(N=k+m)(kk+m​)pkqm by writing e−λ=e−λpe−λq\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda p}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda q}e−λ=e−λpe−λq and λk+m=λkλm\lambda^{k+m}=\lambda^k\lambda^mλk+m=λkλm; the factorials (k+m)!(k+m)!(k+m)! cancel. The Poisson assumption on NNN is what makes the exponential factor split in this way; for other distributions of the total the counts are in general dependent.

The theorem is the basis of Poisson splitting into labelled categories. Read in the other direction, it matches Poisson merging: two independent Poisson counts add up to a Poisson total, and given that total, the first count is binomial.

Key Results¶

  • If Y=N−XY=N-XY=N−X, then X∼Pois⁡(λp)X\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda p)X∼Pois(λp) and Y∼Pois⁡(λ(1−p))Y\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda(1-p))Y∼Pois(λ(1−p)) independently.
