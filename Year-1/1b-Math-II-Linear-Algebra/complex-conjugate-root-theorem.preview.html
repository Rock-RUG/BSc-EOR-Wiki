Complex Conjugate Root Theorem¶

Theorem¶

If PPP is a polynomial in one variable with real coefficients, and a+iba + \mathrm{i} ba+ib is a root of PPP with aaa and bbb being real numbers, then its complex conjugate a−iba - \mathrm{i} ba−ib is also a root of PPP.

Interpretation¶

For a polynomial with real coefficients, the non-real roots come in pairs a±iba\pm\mathrm{i}ba±ib: whenever a+iba+\mathrm{i}ba+ib is a root, so is its mirror image in the real axis.

The four plotted points are the roots of p(z)=z4+2z3+2z2+10z+25p(z)=z^4+2z^3+2z^2+10z+25p(z)=z4+2z3+2z2+10z+25, a polynomial with real coefficients. They form the two conjugate pairs 1±2i1\pm2\mathrm{i}1±2i and −2±i-2\pm\mathrm{i}−2±i, and each dashed segment joins a root to its mirror image. Each pair gives a real quadratic factor: p(z)=(z2−2z+5)(z2+4z+5)p(z)=(z^2-2z+5)(z^2+4z+5)p(z)=(z2−2z+5)(z2+4z+5).

For a non-real root λ\lambdaλ, the product (z−λ)(z−λ‾)=z2−2Re⁡(λ)z+∣λ∣2(z-\lambda)(z-\overline{\lambda})=z^2-2\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)z+|\lambda|^2(z−λ)(z−λ)=z2−2Re(λ)z+∣λ∣2 is a real quadratic factor of PPP, so non-real roots can be split off in pairs. Together with the fundamental theorem of algebra, this shows that every real polynomial is a product of real linear and real quadratic factors, and that a real polynomial of odd degree has a real root, as the Key Results state. The theorem returns later for eigenvalues: the non-real eigenvalues of a real matrix also come in conjugate pairs.

Key Results¶

  • If the degree of a real polynomial is odd, it must have at least one real root.
  • Any real n×nn\times nn×n square matrix with nnn odd (so its characteristic polynomial has odd degree nnn) has at least one real eigenvalue.

  • x−ix-\mathrm{i}x−i has the root i\mathrm{i}i but not −i-\mathrm{i}−i: its coefficients are not all real, so the theorem does not apply.
