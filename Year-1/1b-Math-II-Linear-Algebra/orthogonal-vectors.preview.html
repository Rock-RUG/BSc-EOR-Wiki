Orthogonal Vectors¶

Definition¶

Two vectors x\boldsymbol{x}x and y\boldsymbol{y}y are orthogonal if their inner product is zero: x⋅y=0\boldsymbol{x}\cdot \boldsymbol{y}=0x⋅y=0.

Interpretation¶

Orthogonal vectors meet at a right angle in Euclidean geometry. The inner product turns this geometric condition into a computation: instead of measuring an angle, multiply matching coordinates, add, and check whether the result is 000.

The vectors x=(3,1)\boldsymbol{x}=(3,1)x=(3,1) and y=(−1,3)\boldsymbol{y}=(-1,3)y=(−1,3) in the figure satisfy x⋅y=3⋅(−1)+1⋅3=0\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{y}=3\cdot(-1)+1\cdot3=0x⋅y=3⋅(−1)+1⋅3=0, so the angle between them is 90∘90^\circ90∘. Here y\boldsymbol{y}y is x\boldsymbol{x}x turned a quarter turn; in R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 the vectors (a,b)(a,b)(a,b) and (−b,a)(-b,a)(−b,a) are always orthogonal. Pythagoras holds as well: x+y=(2,4)\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}=(2,4)x+y=(2,4) has ∥x+y∥2=20=10+10=∥x∥2+∥y∥2\|\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}\|^2=20=10+10=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^2∥x+y∥2=20=10+10=∥x∥2+∥y∥2.

The link with Pythagoras holds in every dimension. Expanding gives ∥x+y∥2=∥x∥2+2 x⋅y+∥y∥2\|\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}\|^2=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2+2\,\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{y}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|^2∥x+y∥2=∥x∥2+2x⋅y+∥y∥2, so the Euclidean norms add in squares exactly when x⋅y=0\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{y}=0x⋅y=0. The definition also covers the zero vector: it is orthogonal to every vector, and it is the only vector orthogonal to itself, because x⋅x=∥x∥2\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}=\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2x⋅x=∥x∥2. A right angle only makes sense for nonzero vectors.

Orthogonality defines the normal vector of a plane, and the shortest distance from a point to a line is measured along the perpendicular. It returns later in orthonormal bases, bases of mutually orthogonal vectors of length 111.

Orthogonal should not be confused with linearly independent. Nonzero vectors that are pairwise orthogonal are always independent, but independent vectors need not be orthogonal: (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) and (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) are independent, with inner product 111.

Key Results¶

  • Orthogonality is equivalent to a 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle for nonzero real vectors.
  • If y\boldsymbol{y}y has unit length, then x⋅y\boldsymbol{x}\cdot \boldsymbol{y}x⋅y gives the signed length of the projection of x\boldsymbol{x}x onto y\boldsymbol{y}y.

Examples¶

  • (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) and (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) are orthogonal.
  • (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) and (1,−1)(1,-1)(1,−1) are orthogonal.
