Euclidean Space¶

Definition¶

A Euclidean space is a pair (V,⟨⋅,⋅⟩)(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)(V,⟨⋅,⋅⟩) of a real vector space VVV with dim⁡V<∞\dim V<\inftydimV<∞ and an inner product ⟨⋅,⋅⟩ ⁣:V×V→R\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\colon V\times V\to\mathbb{R}⟨⋅,⋅⟩:V×V→R; in other words, a finite-dimensional real inner product space.

The standard example, and the one meant by the Euclidean space Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn, is the real coordinate space Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn with the dot product x⋅y=x1y1+⋯+xnyn\boldsymbol{x}\cdot \boldsymbol{y}=x_1y_1+\cdots+x_ny_nx⋅y=x1​y1​+⋯+xn​yn​.

Interpretation¶

Euclidean space is the setting for ordinary geometry in linear algebra. A vector space on its own only knows how to add and scale vectors; the inner product adds the measurements, namely lengths, distances and angles.

In R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 with the dot product, every quantity in the figure comes from the inner product: the length ∥x∥=42+12=17\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=\sqrt{4^2+1^2}=\sqrt{17}∥x∥=42+12​=17​, the inner product ⟨x,y⟩=4⋅1+1⋅3=7\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=4\cdot1+1\cdot3=7⟨x,y⟩=4⋅1+1⋅3=7, the angle through cos⁡θ=71710\cos\theta=\dfrac{7}{\sqrt{17}\sqrt{10}}cosθ=17​10​7​, so θ≈57.5∘\theta\approx57.5^\circθ≈57.5∘, and the distance between the tips, d(x,y)=∥(3,−2)∥=13d(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\|(3,-2)\|=\sqrt{13}d(x,y)=∥(3,−2)∥=13​.

Each part of the definition has a role. The space is real because lengths and angles are real numbers. It is finite-dimensional, which makes every Euclidean space look like Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn, as the Key Results explain. And the inner product is part of the data: the same vector space R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 with the inner product 2x1y1+x2y22x_1y_1+x_2y_22x1​y1​+x2​y2​ from the Examples has different lengths and angles, so its unit circle is an ellipse in the usual picture.

In Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn with the dot product, the Euclidean norm, the Euclidean distance, the angle between vectors and orthogonality all come from this one inner product, and so does the description of planes and hyperplanes by normal vectors.

A common slip is to identify Euclidean space with the set Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn. The set alone carries no geometry; it becomes the Euclidean space Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn together with the dot product.

Key Results¶

  • The inner product induces the norm ∥x∥=⟨x,x⟩\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=\sqrt{\langle \boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}∥x∥=⟨x,x⟩​ and the distance d(x,y)=∥x−y∥d(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|d(x,y)=∥x−y∥; on Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn with the dot product these are the Euclidean norm and the Euclidean distance.
  • The inner product also defines the angle between vectors and orthogonality.
  • Every nnn-dimensional Euclidean space is isomorphic to Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn with the dot product by a linear bijection that preserves the inner product: write each vector in coordinates with respect to an orthonormal basis, a basis of mutually orthogonal unit vectors.
  • Lines, planes, and hyperplanes are geometric objects in Euclidean spaces.

Examples¶

  • R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 with the dot product is the Euclidean plane; R3\mathbb{R}^3R3 with the dot product is ordinary three-dimensional Euclidean space.
  • R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 with ⟨x,y⟩=2x1y1+x2y2\langle \boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=2x_1y_1+x_2y_2⟨x,y⟩=2x1​y1​+x2​y2​ is also a Euclidean space; its unit circle {x ⁣:⟨x,x⟩=1}\{\boldsymbol{x}\colon\langle \boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=1\}{x:⟨x,x⟩=1} is an ellipse in the usual picture of the plane.
