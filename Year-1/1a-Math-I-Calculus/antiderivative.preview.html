Antiderivative¶

Definition¶

Let III be an interval, and let f ⁣:I→R.f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}\text{.}f:I→R. A function F ⁣:I→RF\colon I\to\mathbb{R}F:I→R is an antiderivative of fff on III if F′(x)=f(x)F'(x)=f(x)F′(x)=f(x) at every interior point of I,I\text{,}I, with one-sided derivatives at included endpoints.

Interpretation¶

An antiderivative of fff is a function FFF whose derivative is fff, so finding one reverses differentiation: given the slope at every point, recover a function with those slopes.

The figure shows antiderivatives of f(x)=xf(x)=xf(x)=x: the curves F(x)=x22+CF(x)=\tfrac{x^2}{2}+CF(x)=2x2​+C for C=−1,0,1,2C=-1,0,1,2C=−1,0,1,2 are vertical shifts of one another. At x=1x=1x=1 their tangent segments are parallel, all with slope f(1)=1f(1)=1f(1)=1. Shifting a graph up or down does not change its slopes, which is why F+CF+CF+C is again an antiderivative.

The derivative is the test for an antiderivative: differentiating FFF must give fff. At an included endpoint of III only a one-sided derivative is available, so the condition there is one-sided, while at interior points it is two-sided.

Not every function has an antiderivative. The signum function has none on [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1]: an antiderivative would be −x-x−x plus a constant on [−1,0)[-1,0)[−1,0) and xxx plus a constant on (0,1](0,1](0,1], and continuity at 000 would force it to be ∣x∣+C|x|+C∣x∣+C, which is not differentiable at 000. Key Results¶

  • If FFF is an antiderivative of fff on an interval I,I\text{,}I, then so is F+CF + CF+C for any constant C∈R.C \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}C∈R.
  • Antiderivatives are not unique; on an interval, any two antiderivatives of fff differ by a constant.
  • Every continuous function on an interval has an antiderivative, but an integrable function need not have one: the signum function is integrable on [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1] and has no antiderivative there.
