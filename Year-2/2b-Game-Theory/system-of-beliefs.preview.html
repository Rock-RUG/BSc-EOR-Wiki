System of Beliefs¶

Definition¶

In an extensive-form game, a system of beliefs μ\muμ assigns to every information set hhh a probability distribution over the decision nodes in hhh:

μ(x)∈[0,1]  for each x∈h,∑x∈hμ(x)=1.\mu(x)\in[0,1]\ \text{ for each } x\in h,\qquad \sum_{x\in h}\mu(x)=1.μ(x)∈[0,1]  for each x∈h,x∈h∑​μ(x)=1.

The number μ(x)\mu(x)μ(x) is the probability that the player who moves at hhh assigns to being at node xxx, given that hhh has been reached. A strategy profile σ\sigmaσ together with a system of beliefs μ\muμ is called an assessment (σ,μ)(\sigma,\mu)(σ,μ).

Interpretation¶

At an information set with several nodes, a player knows that one of them has been reached but not which. Expected continuation payoffs are calculated by weighting the payoffs at the different nodes. Beliefs supply those weights. The best action can depend on them, although an action that is best at every node can be chosen without knowing the precise belief. They make it possible to define sequential rationality at every information set, including information sets that equilibrium play never reaches.

In the figure (the entry example below), the incumbent's information set after entry is reached with probability 15⋅1=0.2\tfrac{1}{5}\cdot1=0.251​⋅1=0.2 through the competitive type and 45⋅12=0.4\tfrac{4}{5}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}=0.454​⋅21​=0.4 through the weak type. The belief divides these weights by their total 0.60.60.6, giving μ=13\mu=\tfrac{1}{3}μ=31​ on the competitive node and 1−μ=231-\mu=\tfrac{2}{3}1−μ=32​ on the weak one; these are the weights the incumbent uses to compare its actions.

Beliefs describe uncertainty about the history leading to the information set. With perfect recall, this concerns moves the player did not observe, such as Nature's type draw or a rival's action. Without perfect recall it can also concern her own forgotten actions. They are a conditional probability of the node given the information set. A singleton information set carries the trivial belief μ(x)=1\mu(x)=1μ(x)=1, so beliefs only matter at information sets with two or more nodes.

A system of beliefs is part of the description of an equilibrium, not a consequence of payoffs alone. Equilibrium concepts then restrict it: on the equilibrium path, beliefs must follow from the strategies by Bayes' rule; off the path the restriction is weaker, and different beliefs can support different equilibria.

Key Results¶

The on-path conditional-probability formula uses the usual setting in which a play cannot visit two nodes of the same information set, as guaranteed by perfect recall.

  • A system of beliefs specifies one distribution per information set; at a two-node information set it reduces to one number μ∈[0,1]\mu\in[0,1]μ∈[0,1], with 1−μ1-\mu1−μ on the other node.
  • At an information set reached with positive probability under σ\sigmaσ, beliefs consistent with σ\sigmaσ are uniquely determined by Bayes' rule.
  • At an information set reached with probability 000 under σ\sigmaσ, Bayes' conditional ratio is undefined. In the weak perfect Bayesian equilibrium convention used here, Bayes' rule alone leaves any probability distribution admissible; the assessment must still satisfy sequential rationality. A singleton has only the belief 111.
  • In a signalling game, the receiver has one information set per message, and the beliefs are the posterior probabilities of the sender's types after each message.
