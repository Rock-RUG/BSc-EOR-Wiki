Multistage Game¶

Definition¶

A multistage game (with observed actions) is an extensive-form game played in stages t=0,1,…,T−1t=0,1,\dots,T-1t=0,1,…,T−1, where the number of stages TTT may be finite or infinite (then t=0,1,2,…t=0,1,2,\dotst=0,1,2,…). Interpretation¶

The defining assumption is observed actions: at the start of each stage all players know the full history. In the game tree, the node at which stage ttt begins after a given hth^tht forms a singleton information set, so every history starts a subgame. This is what makes subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium tractable here: one checks that the continuation play after every history is an equilibrium.

Conditioning on history is what creates new equilibria. A player can be rewarded with a good stage-game equilibrium tomorrow for playing a non-equilibrium action today, and punished with a bad one if she does not. With finitely many stages this works only if some later stage game has more than one equilibrium; when every stage game has a unique equilibrium there is nothing to condition on. With infinitely many stages, as in an infinitely repeated game, the threat of future punishment can work even with a unique stage equilibrium.

Do not read "multistage" as "players move one after another": within a stage the moves are simultaneous, and the dynamic structure comes from the sequence of stages.

Key Results¶

  • Each history hth^tht with t≤T−1t\le T-1t≤T−1 begins a subgame; for t=0t=0t=0 the empty history gives the whole game, which is a subgame but not a proper subgame, and every t≥1t\ge1t≥1 gives a proper subgame.
  • Playing a stage-game Nash equilibrium of GtG_tGt​ in each stage, independently of history, is a subgame-perfect equilibrium.
  • If TTT is finite and every GtG_tGt​ has a unique Nash equilibrium, the game has a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, obtained by backward induction: play that equilibrium in every stage after every history.
  • If some later stage game has two equilibria with different payoffs, a non-equilibrium profile can be sustained in earlier stages, provided each player's payoff difference between the two equilibria outweighs her one-stage gain from deviating.
  • Subgame perfection can be checked one stage at a time by the one-stage deviation principle.
