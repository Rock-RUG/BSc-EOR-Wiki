Prisoner's Dilemma¶

Definition¶

The Prisoner's Dilemma is a symmetric two-player normal-form game ⟨N,{Si},{vi}⟩\langle N,\{S_i\},\{v_i\}\rangle⟨N,{Si​},{vi​}⟩ with N={1,2}N=\{1,2\}N={1,2} and S1=S2={M,F}S_1=S_2=\{M,F\}S1​=S2​={M,F} (stay Mum, Fink on the other) with two properties:

  1. for each player, FFF gives a strictly higher payoff than MMM whatever the other player does, so FFF is a strictly dominant strategy;
  2. both players get strictly more when both play MMM than when both play FFF.

The story: two suspects are questioned in separate rooms; each can stay mum or fink. A standard numerical version, with payoffs equal to minus the years in prison:

MMM FFF
MMM −2,−2-2,-2−2,−2 −5,−1-5,-1−5,−1
FFF −1,−5-1,-5−1,−5 −4,−4-4,-4−4,−4

Interpretation¶

The game is the standard example of a conflict between individual and collective rationality. Each player's reasoning is airtight: whatever the other does, finking shortens her own sentence. Yet when both follow it they reach (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) with (−4,−4)(-4,-4)(−4,−4), while (M,M)(M,M)(M,M) would give both −2-2−2. The unique Nash equilibrium is therefore not Pareto efficient.

Start at mutual silence (M,M)(M,M)(M,M) with −2-2−2 each. Player 1 alone gains 111 by finking (−1>−2-1>-2−1>−2, blue arrow), and player 2 alone gains 111 in the same way (red arrow); the same gain of 111 appears in the other row and column, which is why FFF is strictly dominant. When both follow this reasoning they end in the shaded equilibrium (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) with −4-4−4 each, which is 222 worse for each player than the −2-2−2 they could have had at (M,M)(M,M)(M,M).

The same structure appears in price competition (compare the Bertrand duopoly), arms races, contributions to public goods and the overuse of common resources. In those settings MMM is usually called cooperate (CCC) and FFF defect (DDD).

The dilemma does not come from a lack of information or of communication. The game is a static game of complete information, so each player knows all payoffs; a promise to stay mum before the game does not change the fact that FFF is better afterwards. Changing the outcome requires changing the game, for example through enforceable contracts or, later in the course, through repeated interaction.

The prediction is also unusually robust. Because FFF is strictly dominant, a player needs no belief about the other player, and no assumption that the other is rational, to conclude that she should fink. In games such as the Battle of the Sexes, by contrast, each player's best choice depends on what she expects the other to do.

Compare it with the chicken game. There no action is dominant, mutual toughness is the worst outcome, and there are three equilibria. In the Prisoner's Dilemma mutual defection is bad but each player still prefers it to being the only one who cooperates, and there is a single equilibrium.

Key Results¶

  • (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) is the unique Nash equilibrium, and it is a strictly dominant strategy equilibrium.
  • Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies removes MMM for both players in the first round, because MMM is a strictly dominated strategy.
  • (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) is Pareto dominated by (M,M)(M,M)(M,M); the other three profiles are Pareto efficient. In the table, (M,M)(M,M)(M,M) also has the largest total payoff, −4-4−4, against −6-6−6 for one-sided finking and −8-8−8 for (F,F)(F,F)(F,F).
  • Both defining properties are needed. If mutual MMM were not better for both than mutual FFF, the unique equilibrium would be efficient and there would be no dilemma; if FFF were not dominant, the game would no longer force (F,F)(F,F)(F,F).
  • With mixed strategies (Week 2), (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) is still the unique Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies, because a strictly dominated strategy gets probability 000 in every equilibrium.

Looking ahead to Week 4: in a finitely repeated game with the Prisoner's Dilemma as stage game, the unique subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium plays (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) in every period. In the infinitely repeated game, mutual cooperation can be sustained by the grim trigger strategy if the discount factor δ\deltaδ is large enough.
