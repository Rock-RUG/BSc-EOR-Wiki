Interpretation¶

The action does not fix the outcome; it changes the distribution of outcomes. The decision maker compares actions through the expectation of the payoff, computed from the probability mass function p(⋅∣a)p(\cdot\mid a)p(⋅∣a) or the probability density function f(⋅∣a)f(\cdot\mid a)f(⋅∣a). Computing E[v(x)∣a]\mathbb{E}[v(x)\mid a]E[v(x)∣a] from the distribution of xxx, without first finding the distribution of v(x)v(x)v(x), is LOTUS, in the discrete and the continuous case alike.

Here the payoff values are cardinal: differences between payoffs matter, not only their order. A common misunderstanding is that the rule means maximizing expected money. That holds only when v(x)=xv(x)=xv(x)=x. If vvv is a strictly concave utility of money, the same rule is maximization of expected utility and describes a risk-averse decision maker (see expected utility and risk aversion).

In games, the uncertainty often comes from the other players: a belief about their choices plays the role of p(⋅∣a)p(\cdot\mid a)p(⋅∣a), and from Week 2 a mixed strategy creates such uncertainty deliberately.

Key Results¶

  • The expected payoff is linear in the probabilities: for a compound lottery that runs lottery L1L_1L1​ with probability λ\lambdaλ and L2L_2L2​ with probability 1−λ1-\lambda1−λ, the expected payoff is λ EL1[v]+(1−λ) EL2[v]\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{L_1}[v]+(1-\lambda)\,\mathbb{E}_{L_2}[v]λEL1​​[v]+(1−λ)EL2​​[v].
  • Replacing vvv by αv+β\alpha v+\betaαv+β with α>0\alpha>0α>0 leaves the ranking of actions unchanged; a nonlinear strictly increasing transformation can reverse it (Example 1).
  • If AAA is finite, a maximizing action exists.
  • The expected payoff of an action lies between the smallest and largest payoff among the outcomes it can produce.
  • If every action leads to a single outcome with probability 111, the rule reduces to choice under certainty.
