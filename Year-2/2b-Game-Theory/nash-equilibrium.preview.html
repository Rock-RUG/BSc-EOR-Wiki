Nash Equilibrium¶

Definition¶

Interpretation¶

At a Nash equilibrium no player can gain by changing strategy alone. Each player's belief about the others is correct, and each player acts optimally given that belief, so the profile is self-enforcing: once it is expected, nobody wants to leave it.

In the figure, player 1's payoff is boxed when it is the largest in its column and player 2's when it is the largest in its row. A cell with both payoffs boxed is a profile in which each strategy is a best response to the other, so neither player gains by moving alone: (O,O)(O,O)(O,O) and (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) are the pure Nash equilibria, while from (O,F)(O,F)(O,F) or (F,O)(F,O)(F,O) either player would switch.

Only unilateral deviations are tested. A profile that every player would like to leave together can still be an equilibrium, as (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) in the Prisoner's Dilemma shows. Hence an equilibrium need not be Pareto efficient, and a profile that maximizes total payoff need not be an equilibrium.

Nash equilibrium does not require that strategies be dominant: each si∗s_i^*si∗​ only needs to be optimal against s−i∗s_{-i}^*s−i∗​, not against everything.

Key Results¶

  • Best-response marking method (finite two-player games). In each column, mark player 1's highest payoff (all of them if tied); in each row, mark player 2's highest payoff (all of them if tied). A cell is a pure Nash equilibrium if and only if both of its payoffs are marked, since a marked first entry means s1∈BR1(s2)s_1\in BR_1(s_2)s1​∈BR1​(s2​) and a marked second entry means s2∈BR2(s1)s_2\in BR_2(s_1)s2​∈BR2​(s1​).
  • A strictly dominant strategy equilibrium is the unique Nash equilibrium.
  • Every Nash equilibrium survives iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies; in a finite game, if that procedure leaves one profile, it is the unique Nash equilibrium.
  • A pure-strategy Nash equilibrium may not exist (Matching Pennies) or may not be unique (Battle of the Sexes). Every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies by the Nash existence theorem (Week 2).
