Battle of the Sexes¶

Definition¶

The Battle of the Sexes is a two-player normal-form game in which each player chooses OOO or FFF (traditionally opera or football). Both players want to choose the same option, but player 1 prefers that they meet at OOO and player 2 that they meet at FFF; failing to coordinate gives both the lowest payoff. The standard numerical version:

OOO FFF
OOO 2,12,12,1 0,00,00,0
FFF 0,00,00,0 1,21,21,2

Interpretation¶

Battle of the Sexes is a coordination game with a conflict of interest. Matching is good for both, but the two ways of matching split the gains differently. It models situations such as two firms choosing a common technical standard, each preferring its own.

Each point is a payoff pair (v1,v2)(v_1,v_2)(v1​,v2​) from the table. The two pure equilibria (filled green) are both far better than miscoordination at (0,0)(0,0)(0,0), but player 1 prefers (O,O)(O,O)(O,O) with (2,1)(2,1)(2,1) and player 2 prefers (F,F)(F,F)(F,F) with (1,2)(1,2)(1,2), so neither point is better for both. The mixed equilibrium of Week 2 (open circle, see Key Results) gives each player 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​; both pure equilibria lie in the shaded quadrant of pairs that are higher for both players, which is the sense in which the mixed equilibrium is Pareto dominated by each of them.

The game has more than one Nash equilibrium, and the theory alone does not say which one is played. Each pure equilibrium is self-enforcing once both players expect it, but nothing in the payoffs tells the players which of the two to expect. Common knowledge of rationality does not help either: no strategy is strictly dominated, so dominance reasoning eliminates nothing. In practice coordination may come from convention, communication or the order of moves.

Each player's best response is to do what the other does, so the right choice depends entirely on the belief about the other player. This is the opposite of the Prisoner's Dilemma, where finking is best whatever the other does. Unlike in the Prisoner's Dilemma, both pure equilibria here are efficient: the players disagree about which coordinated outcome to choose. The mixed equilibrium is Pareto dominated, as the figure shows.

Compare it with two other 2×22\times22×2 games. In Matching Pennies (Week 2) one player wants to match and the other to mismatch, so there is no pure equilibrium at all. In the chicken game both have two pure equilibria and one mixed equilibrium, but in chicken each player wants to do the opposite of the other, while here each wants to do the same.

Key Results¶

  • The pure Nash equilibria are (O,O)(O,O)(O,O) and (F,F)(F,F)(F,F); the two mismatches are not equilibria.
  • The best responses are BR1(O)=OBR_1(O)=OBR1​(O)=O, BR1(F)=FBR_1(F)=FBR1​(F)=F, BR2(O)=OBR_2(O)=OBR2​(O)=O and BR2(F)=FBR_2(F)=FBR2​(F)=F.
  • No player has a dominant strategy and no strategy is strictly dominated, so iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies removes nothing.
  • Both pure equilibria are Pareto efficient and cannot be ranked against each other: player 1 prefers (O,O)(O,O)(O,O) and player 2 prefers (F,F)(F,F)(F,F). Both give the same total payoff, 333.
  • In a pure coordination game, where both players prefer the same matched outcome, one equilibrium would Pareto dominate the other; in Battle of the Sexes neither does.

Looking ahead:

  • With mixed strategies (Week 2) there is exactly one further Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies: player 1 plays OOO with probability 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​ and player 2 plays OOO with probability 13\tfrac{1}{3}31​, giving each an expected payoff of 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​. It is Pareto dominated by both pure equilibria.
  • If player 1 moves first and player 2 observes the move (Week 3), the unique subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium leads to (O,O)(O,O)(O,O): the first mover gets her preferred outcome.

Examples¶

Pure equilibria. Check best responses cell by cell. Against OOO, player 1 prefers OOO (2>2>02>00); against FFF, she prefers FFF (1>1>01>00). Player 2 likewise wants to match player 1 (1>1>01>00 against OOO, 2>02>02>0 against FFF). The cells where both players are best responding are (O,O)(O,O)(O,O) and (F,F)(F,F)(F,F). At (O,F)(O,F)(O,F) either player gains by switching, so it is not an equilibrium.

A belief decides the choice. If player 1 believes that player 2 goes to OOO with probability qqq, her expected payoffs are 2q2q2q from OOO and 1−q1-q1−q from FFF. She prefers OOO exactly when 2q>1−q2q>1-q2q>1−q, that is, q>13q>\tfrac{1}{3}q>31​. Even a fairly small chance that player 2 comes to the opera is enough, because player 1 gains more from meeting there.

The mixed equilibrium (Week 2). Let ppp be player 1's probability of OOO and qqq player 2's. By the indifference principle, each player's mixture makes the other indifferent:

Each player expects 23\tfrac{2}{3}32​, less than the worse of her two pure-equilibrium payoffs. The players miscoordinate with probability 23⋅23+13⋅13=59\tfrac{2}{3}\cdot\tfrac{2}{3}+\tfrac{1}{3}\cdot\tfrac{1}{3}=\tfrac{5}{9}32​⋅32​+31​⋅31​=95​.

Sequential version (Week 3). Suppose player 1 chooses first and player 2 observes the choice. Player 2's strategies are pairs (reply to OOO, reply to FFF). By backward induction she replies OOO to OOO (1>1>01>00) and FFF to FFF (2>2>02>00), the strategy OFOFOF. Anticipating this, player 1 compares 222 from OOO with 111 from FFF and chooses OOO. The SPNE is (O,OF)(O,OF)(O,OF) with outcome (O,O)(O,O)(O,O) and payoffs (2,1)(2,1)(2,1). The profile (F,FF)(F,FF)(F,FF) is still a Nash equilibrium with outcome (F,F)(F,F)(F,F), but it rests on player 2's non-credible threat to choose FFF after OOO.
