Matching Pennies¶

Definition¶

Matching Pennies is a two-player normal-form game in which each player shows a coin, HHH (heads) or TTT (tails), simultaneously. Player 1 wins if the coins match and player 2 wins if they differ; the winner gets 111 and the loser −1-1−1:

HHH TTT
HHH 1,−11,-11,−1 −1,1-1,1−1,1
TTT −1,1-1,1−1,1 1,−11,-11,−1

The game is zero-sum: v1(s)+v2(s)=0v_1(s)+v_2(s)=0v1​(s)+v2​(s)=0 for every profile sss.

Interpretation¶

Matching Pennies is the simplest game of pure conflict in which being predictable is costly. If player 2 knew player 1's choice, she would pick the other side and win; the same holds the other way round. It models situations such as a penalty kick (shoot left or right, dive left or right) or a tax inspection (evade or comply, inspect or not).

There is no Nash equilibrium in pure strategies: in every cell the loser wants to switch. The game therefore shows why mixed strategies are needed. In the equilibrium each player randomizes 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​–12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, which leaves the opponent with nothing to exploit.

In each cell the arrow shows the loser's profitable switch. At (H,H)(H,H)(H,H) player 2 moves to TTT (red, along the row); at (H,T)(H,T)(H,T) player 1 moves to TTT (blue, down the column); at (T,T)(T,T)(T,T) player 2 moves back to HHH; at (T,H)(T,H)(T,H) player 1 moves back to HHH. The four arrows close into a cycle, so play never settles on a pure profile, and the only equilibrium is the mixed one in which both players play HHH with probability 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​.

The equilibrium probabilities are not chosen to maximize a player's own payoff; any mixture is a best response once the opponent mixes equally. They are pinned down by the requirement that the opponent is indifferent. If player 2 played HHH with probability q>12q>\tfrac{1}{2}q>21​, player 1's unique best response would be the pure strategy HHH, and that cannot be part of an equilibrium.

Drawn as a game tree (Week 3), player 2's two decision nodes form one information set, because she does not see player 1's coin. If she could see it, she would always win.

Key Results¶

  • Matching Pennies has no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium.
  • No strategy is dominated, so iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies removes nothing.
  • It has a unique Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies, in which both players play HHH with probability 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​; each player's expected payoff is 000. The equilibrium is not strict: against the opponent's mixture, HHH, TTT and every mixture give the same payoff.
  • The best-response correspondence of player 1 is HHH if q>12q>\tfrac{1}{2}q>21​, TTT if q<12q<\tfrac{1}{2}q<21​ and every mixture if q=12q=\tfrac{1}{2}q=21​, where qqq is player 2's probability of HHH; player 2's is the mirror image. The two graphs cross only at (12,12)(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})(21​,21​).
  • The equilibrium mixture guarantees each player an expected payoff of 000 whatever the opponent does.
  • The game shows that existence of a pure equilibrium cannot be guaranteed, while the Nash existence theorem guarantees an equilibrium in mixed strategies.

Examples¶

No pure equilibrium. At (H,H)(H,H)(H,H) and (T,T)(T,T)(T,T) player 2 loses and gains 222 by switching; at (H,T)(H,T)(H,T) and (T,H)(T,H)(T,H) player 1 loses and gains 222 by switching. No cell survives.

The mixed equilibrium. Let player 1 play HHH with probability ppp and player 2 with probability qqq. By the indifference principle,

v1(H,σ2)=q−(1−q)=2q−1,v1(T,σ2)=−q+(1−q)=1−2q,\begin{aligned} v_1(H,\sigma_2)&=q-(1-q)=2q-1,\\ v_1(T,\sigma_2)&=-q+(1-q)=1-2q, \end{aligned}v1​(H,σ2​)v1​(T,σ2​)​=q−(1−q)=2q−1,=−q+(1−q)=1−2q,​

which are equal only at q=12q=\tfrac{1}{2}q=21​; symmetrically p=12p=\tfrac{1}{2}p=21​. With p=12p=\tfrac{1}{2}p=21​, player 1's payoff is 12(2q−1)+12(1−2q)=0\tfrac{1}{2}(2q-1)+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-2q)=021​(2q−1)+21​(1−2q)=0 for every qqq.

Why other mixtures fail. Suppose player 2 plays HHH with probability q=0.7q=0.7q=0.7. Then HHH gives player 1 2(0.7)−1=0.42(0.7)-1=0.42(0.7)−1=0.4 and TTT gives −0.4-0.4−0.4, so her unique best response is the pure strategy HHH. Against HHH, player 2's best response is TTT, which contradicts q=0.7q=0.7q=0.7. The same argument rules out every q≠12q\neq\tfrac{1}{2}q=21​, and symmetrically every p≠12p\neq\tfrac{1}{2}p=21​.

An unbalanced version. Suppose player 1 wins 222 (and player 2 loses 222) at (H,H)(H,H)(H,H), all other payoffs unchanged. Player 1 is indifferent when 2q−(1−q)=−q+(1−q)2q-(1-q)=-q+(1-q)2q−(1−q)=−q+(1−q), so q=25q=\tfrac{2}{5}q=52​. Player 2 is indifferent when −2p+(1−p)=p−(1−p)-2p+(1-p)=p-(1-p)−2p+(1−p)=p−(1−p), so p=25p=\tfrac{2}{5}p=52​. Player 1's expected payoff is 3q−1=153q-1=\tfrac{1}{5}3q−1=51​. The larger prize at (H,H)(H,H)(H,H) changes player 2's mixture, who now avoids HHH, and also player 1's: she plays HHH less often than before, because her probability is set by player 2's payoffs, not her own.
