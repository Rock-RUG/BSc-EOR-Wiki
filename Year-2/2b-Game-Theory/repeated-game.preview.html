Interpretation¶

Repetition lets today's behaviour be rewarded or punished tomorrow. Whether this matters depends on the horizon. With a known last period, nothing that happens later can influence play in that period, so it must be a stage-game Nash equilibrium; if that equilibrium is unique, the argument unravels backwards to the first period. With an infinite horizon there is no last period, and cooperation can be enforced by threats such as the grim trigger strategy.

The discount factor δ\deltaδ measures patience. It can also be read as the probability that the relationship continues for another period, so "infinite" means "no known end date", not "literally forever". Large δ\deltaδ makes future losses weigh heavily against a one-period gain.

Key Results¶

  • If GGG has a unique Nash equilibrium a∗a^*a∗ and T<∞T<\inftyT<∞, the unique subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium plays a∗a^*a∗ in every period after every history.
  • For any TTT and δ\deltaδ, repeating a fixed stage-game Nash equilibrium regardless of history is subgame perfect.
  • In the infinitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma of the Examples, mutual cooperation is the outcome of a subgame-perfect equilibrium in grim trigger strategies if and only if δ≥(5−3)/(5−1)=12\delta\ge (5-3)/(5-1)=\tfrac{1}{2}δ≥(5−3)/(5−1)=21​: the one-period gain from defecting against a cooperator, divided by the difference between that defection payoff and the payoff of mutual defection.
  • The folk theorem: for δ\deltaδ close to 1, every feasible payoff vector giving each player strictly more than the payoff of a Nash equilibrium of the stage game is the average payoff of a subgame-perfect equilibrium.
