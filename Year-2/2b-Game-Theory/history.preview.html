History in a Multistage Game¶

Definition¶

Interpretation¶

The history is the "state" on which players condition: it records who did what, not only payoffs. Because actions are observed by everyone, all players know the same hth^tht, which is why it is also called the public history.

A strategy in an extensive-form game must specify an action at every information set, including those never reached. In a multistage game these information sets are exactly the histories, so a strategy must say what to do after histories that the strategy itself rules out, for example after one's own past deviation. This is what makes threats such as punishment phases part of the strategy, and it is where subgame perfection bites.

A common slip is to index the history by the stage just completed. Time starts at t=0t=0t=0, and hth^tht is the history up to stage ttt: the information available when choosing in stage ttt, so it has ttt entries, a0a^0a0 to at−1a^{t-1}at−1.

Key Results¶

  • Each history hth^tht (for ttt up to the last stage) is the starting point of a subgame; h0h^0h0 gives the whole game, which is a subgame but not a proper subgame.
  • In a TTT-period repeated game, with periods t=0,…,T−1t=0,\dots,T-1t=0,…,T−1, the number of non-terminal histories is ∑t=0T−1∣A∣t\displaystyle \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}|A|^{t}t=0∑T−1​∣A∣t.
  • The number of pure strategies of player iii in a TTT-period repeated game is ∣Ai∣|A_i|∣Ai​∣ raised to the number of non-terminal histories.
  • An outcome path (a0,a1,… )(a^0,a^1,\dots)(a0,a1,…) is one realized history; a strategy profile is not determined by its outcome path, since it also prescribes play after every other history.
  • A strategy that ignores hth^tht (the same action after every history) is history-independent.
