Common-Value Auction¶

Definition¶

In a common-value auction the object has the same value vvv for every bidder, but no bidder knows vvv. Each bidder iii privately observes a signal θi\theta_iθi​ that is informative about vvv, and her payoff from winning at price ppp is v−pv-pv−p. Bidder iii's expected value therefore depends on the other bidders' signals as well as on her own.

A two-bidder example with this structure has v=θ1+θ2v=\theta_1+\theta_2v=θ1​+θ2​, where bidder iii observes only her own component θi\theta_iθi​, and θ1,θ2\theta_1,\theta_2θ1​,θ2​ are independent.

Interpretation¶

The contrast with a private-value auction is about information, not about who values the object more. With private values, learning a rival's signal would not change one's own valuation; with a common value it would. Classic examples are drilling rights to an oil field of unknown size, or a jar of coins whose total is estimated by eye.

In the additive example below, the equilibrium bids are strictly increasing, so a bidder wins when her signal exceeds the rival's. Because the common value increases with the rival's signal, winning lowers its conditional expected value for interior own signals. Common values make the rival's information relevant; the direction of this update depends on the signal model. A rational bidder therefore bids on the basis of the conditional expectation of vvv given her signal and given that she wins (or, in a second-price auction, given that she is just tied). Ignoring this produces the winner's curse.

Truthful bidding results for private values do not carry over directly. In a second-price auction the case argument for bidding "one's value" fails, because one's value is not known; the equilibrium bid must instead be computed as a Bayesian Nash equilibrium.
