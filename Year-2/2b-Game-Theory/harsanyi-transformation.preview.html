Harsanyi Transformation¶

Method¶

The Harsanyi transformation turns a game of incomplete information (players unsure of each other's payoffs) into a game of complete but imperfect information that can be drawn as an extensive-form game:

  1. Add a non-strategic player, Nature, who moves first and draws the type profile θ=(θ1,…,θn)\theta=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n)θ=(θ1​,…,θn​) according to the common prior ppp.
  2. Reveal to each player iii only her own type θi\theta_iθi​: put into one information set of player iii all nodes that differ only in the others' types (and in moves she does not observe).
  3. Let the players then move as in the original game, with payoffs vi(a;θ)v_i(a;\theta)vi​(a;θ) at the terminal nodes.

The resulting game, with the tree, ppp and the information structure common knowledge, is the Bayesian game in extensive form.

Interpretation¶

Analysing a game in which players do not know each other's payoffs seems to need beliefs about beliefs about beliefs. The transformation replaces this regress by a single chance move whose probabilities everyone knows, so uncertainty about the game becomes uncertainty about Nature's move, which the theory of imperfect information already handles.

The figure draws the contribution game of the Examples, where its nodes and information sets are counted. Nature moves first and picks one of the four cost profiles, each with probability 14\tfrac{1}{4}41​; dashed lines join the nodes of one information set, at player 1's level and at player 2's.

A strategy in the transformed game assigns an action to every information set, that is, to every type: it is exactly a type-contingent strategy si ⁣:Θi→Ais_i\colon\Theta_i\to A_isi​:Θi​→Ai​. A Nash equilibrium of the transformed game, with all types having positive probability, is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium.

The assumption that does the work is the common prior: Nature's probabilities must be the same for all players and common knowledge. Without it the players would disagree about Nature's probabilities, and there would be no single commonly known tree.

Key Results¶

  • Player iii has one information set per type at each of her decision points, so her strategies are type-contingent plans.
  • Because Nature's move is hidden from at least one player, the transformed game typically has few proper subgames. In a static Bayesian game where every player has at least two types and sees only her own, the whole game is the only subgame, so subgame perfection adds nothing to BNE.
  • Sequential games of incomplete information, such as the signalling game, are built the same way: Nature first, then moves with partially observed histories.
  • The construction requires no change to payoffs; only the order of moves and the information sets are new.

Examples¶

Contribution game. Each of two players has cost θi∈{14,2}\theta_i\in\{\tfrac{1}{4},2\}θi​∈{41​,2}, independently with probability 12\tfrac{1}{2}21​, and chooses CCC (contribute) or DDD (decline) simultaneously.

  • Nature has 4 branches, one per cost profile, each with probability 14\tfrac{1}{4}41​.
  • Player 1 then moves at 4 nodes; she knows θ1\theta_1θ1​ but not θ2\theta_2θ2​, so the nodes form 2 information sets of 2 nodes each.
  • Player 2 moves at 4×2=84\times2=84×2=8 nodes; she knows θ2\theta_2θ2​ but sees neither θ1\theta_1θ1​ nor player 1's action, so there are 2 information sets of 4 nodes each, one for θ2=14\theta_2=\tfrac{1}{4}θ2​=41​ and one for θ2=2\theta_2=2θ2​=2.
  • There are 4×2×2=164\times2\times2=164×2×2=16 terminal nodes, and each player has 22=42^2=422=4 pure strategies.

Used cars. Nature draws the car's quality LLL or HHH; the seller observes it, the buyer does not, so each pair of buyer decision nodes that follow "low" and "high" with the same observed seller behaviour forms one information set. This is the tree behind the market for lemons.
