Trigger Strategy¶

Definition¶

In a repeated game, a trigger strategy for player iii prescribes a target action aica_i^caic​ (the cooperative phase) as long as the history shows no deviation from the target profile aca^cac since the cooperative phase began, and switches to a punishment phase once a deviation has occurred. In the punishment phase the players follow a specified punishment path, typically a stage-game Nash equilibrium a∗a^*a∗ played either forever (the grim trigger strategy) or for kkk periods, after which play returns to aca^cac.

Interpretation¶

A trigger strategy turns the future into a hostage: a player who grabs a one-period gain loses the difference between cooperative and punishment payoffs in later periods. Deviation is unprofitable when the discounted future loss exceeds the immediate gain, which requires enough patience δ\deltaδ and a punishment that is severe or long enough.

Two separate conditions must hold for a subgame-perfect equilibrium. On the cooperative path, nobody should gain by deviating. In the punishment phase, carrying out the punishment must itself be optimal; a threat to play something that is not a best response once the trigger has been pulled is not credible. An absorbing stage-game Nash punishment takes care of the second condition. For finite punishment the continuation rule also matters. In the example below, every deviation restarts all kkk punishment periods; a stage Nash punishment with payoffs no higher than the target then makes punishment-phase deviations unprofitable.

A trigger strategy reacts to deviations, not to bad luck: it relies on actions being observed. When punishment payoffs are below the target, shorter punishments reduce the future loss and can require a higher δ\deltaδ; a sufficiently short punishment may fail for every δ<1\delta<1δ<1.
