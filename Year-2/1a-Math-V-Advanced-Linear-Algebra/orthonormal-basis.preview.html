Orthonormal Basis¶

Definition¶

Let VVV be a real vector space with an inner product, written x⋅y\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{y}x⋅y (in Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn: the dot product). Vectors q1,…,qk∈V\boldsymbol{q}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_k\in Vq1​,…,qk​∈V are orthonormal (form an orthonormal set) if they are pairwise orthogonal and each has norm 111:

qi⋅qj={1,i=j,0,i≠j.\boldsymbol{q}_i\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_j=\begin{cases}1,&i=j,\\0,&i\neq j.\end{cases}qi​⋅qj​={1,0,​i=j,i=j.​

An orthonormal basis of VVV is an orthonormal set that is also a basis of VVV.

Interpretation¶

An orthonormal basis is a coordinate system with perpendicular axes and unit length along each axis, like the standard basis e1,…,en\boldsymbol{e}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_ne1​,…,en​ of Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn but possibly rotated or reflected. Its advantage is that coordinates no longer require solving a system: the iii-th coordinate of x\boldsymbol{x}x is simply x⋅qi\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_ix⋅qi​, the signed length of the orthogonal projection of x\boldsymbol{x}x onto the line through qi\boldsymbol{q}_iqi​.

The figure uses the first example below: q1=12(1,1)⊤\boldsymbol{q}_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^{\top}q1​=2​1​(1,1)⊤ (blue) and q2=12(1,−1)⊤\boldsymbol{q}_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,-1)^{\top}q2​=2​1​(1,−1)⊤ (red) end on the unit circle and meet at a right angle. Dropping perpendiculars from x=(3,1)⊤\boldsymbol{x}=(3,1)^{\top}x=(3,1)⊤ onto the two lines gives the feet 22 q1=(2,2)⊤2\sqrt{2}\,\boldsymbol{q}_1=(2,2)^{\top}22​q1​=(2,2)⊤ and 2 q2=(1,−1)⊤\sqrt{2}\,\boldsymbol{q}_2=(1,-1)^{\top}2​q2​=(1,−1)⊤, so the coordinates are read off as the inner products x⋅q1=22\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_1=2\sqrt{2}x⋅q1​=22​ and x⋅q2=2\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_2=\sqrt{2}x⋅q2​=2​, and the origin, the two feet and x\boldsymbol{x}x are the corners of a rectangle. No system of equations is solved.

The two conditions do different jobs. Orthogonality alone gives an orthogonal set, whose coordinates are (x⋅vi)/(vi⋅vi)(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_i)/(\boldsymbol{v}_i\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_i)(x⋅vi​)/(vi​⋅vi​); dividing each vi\boldsymbol{v}_ivi​ by ∥vi∥\|\boldsymbol{v}_i\|∥vi​∥ (normalizing) removes the denominators. Unit length alone is not enough: (1,0)⊤(1,0)^{\top}(1,0)⊤ and 12(1,1)⊤\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^{\top}2​1​(1,1)⊤ are unit vectors but not orthogonal.

In matrix form, put q1,…,qk∈Rn\boldsymbol{q}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_k\in\mathbb{R}^nq1​,…,qk​∈Rn as the columns of an n×kn\times kn×k matrix QQQ. The (i,j)(i,j)(i,j) entry of Q⊤QQ^{\top}QQ⊤Q is qi⋅qj\boldsymbol{q}_i\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_jqi​⋅qj​, so the columns are orthonormal exactly when Q⊤Q=IkQ^{\top}Q=I_kQ⊤Q=Ik​. When k=nk=nk=n, QQQ is an orthogonal matrix and is the transformation matrix of the basis in the sense of change of basis; its inverse is simply Q⊤Q^{\top}Q⊤.

Key Results¶

  • An orthonormal set is linearly independent: taking the inner product of ∑iciqi=0\displaystyle \sum_i c_i\boldsymbol{q}_i=\boldsymbol{0}i∑​ci​qi​=0 with qj\boldsymbol{q}_jqj​ gives cj=0c_j=0cj​=0. The same argument works for any orthogonal set of nonzero vectors. Hence an orthonormal set in an nnn-dimensional space has at most nnn vectors, and nnn orthonormal vectors form an orthonormal basis.
  • Expansion: if q1,…,qn\boldsymbol{q}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_nq1​,…,qn​ is an orthonormal basis of VVV, every x∈V\boldsymbol{x}\in Vx∈V satisfies x=∑i=1n(x⋅qi) qi\displaystyle \boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{i=1}^n(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_i)\,\boldsymbol{q}_ix=i=1∑n​(x⋅qi​)qi​; in Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn the coordinate vector is y=Q⊤x\boldsymbol{y}=Q^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}y=Q⊤x.
  • Lengths and inner products can be computed in coordinates: x⋅z=∑i(x⋅qi)(z⋅qi)\displaystyle \boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}=\sum_i(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_i)(\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_i)x⋅z=i∑​(x⋅qi​)(z⋅qi​) and ∥x∥2=∑i(x⋅qi)2\displaystyle \|\boldsymbol{x}\|^2=\sum_i(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_i)^2∥x∥2=i∑​(x⋅qi​)2 (Parseval's identity). With respect to a basis that is not orthonormal, neither formula holds.
  • Existence: every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis, and every orthonormal set extends to one; the Gram-Schmidt algorithm constructs it from any basis.
  • If q1,…,qk\boldsymbol{q}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_kq1​,…,qk​ is an orthonormal basis of a subspace WWW of Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn, then proj⁡Wx=∑i=1k(x⋅qi)qi=QQ⊤x\displaystyle \operatorname{proj}_W\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{i=1}^k(\boldsymbol{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{q}_i)\boldsymbol{q}_i=QQ^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}projW​x=i=1∑k​(x⋅qi​)qi​=QQ⊤x; for k<nk<nk<n, QQ⊤≠InQQ^{\top}\neq I_nQQ⊤=In​ even though Q⊤Q=IkQ^{\top}Q=I_kQ⊤Q=Ik​.
  • If q1,…,qk\boldsymbol{q}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_kq1​,…,qk​ is extended to an orthonormal basis q1,…,qn\boldsymbol{q}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_nq1​,…,qn​ of Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn, the added vectors qk+1,…,qn\boldsymbol{q}_{k+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{q}_nqk+1​,…,qn​ form an orthonormal basis of the orthogonal complement W⊥W^{\perp}W⊥, so dim⁡W+dim⁡W⊥=n\dim W+\dim W^{\perp}=ndimW+dimW⊥=n.
