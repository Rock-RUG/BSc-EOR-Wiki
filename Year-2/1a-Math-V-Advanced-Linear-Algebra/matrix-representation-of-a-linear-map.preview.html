Matrix Representation of a Linear Map¶

Definition¶

Let VVV and WWW be finite-dimensional vector spaces over the same field, with an ordered basis B=(v1,…,vn)\mathcal{B}=(\boldsymbol{v}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_n)B=(v1​,…,vn​) of VVV and an ordered basis C=(w1,…,wm)\mathcal{C}=(\boldsymbol{w}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_m)C=(w1​,…,wm​) of WWW. For a linear map A ⁣:V→WA\colon V\to WA:V→W, write each image of a basis vector in the basis C\mathcal{C}C:

Avj=∑i=1maij wi,j=1,…,n.A\boldsymbol{v}_j=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{ij}\,\boldsymbol{w}_i,\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.Avj​=i=1∑m​aij​wi​,j=1,…,n.

The matrix representation of AAA with respect to B\mathcal{B}B and C\mathcal{C}C is the m×nm\times nm×n matrix [A]C,B=(aij)[A]_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B}}=(a_{ij})[A]C,B​=(aij​). Its jjj-th column is the coordinate vector [Avj]C[A\boldsymbol{v}_j]_{\mathcal{C}}[Avj​]C​. When V=WV=WV=W and B=C\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}B=C, one writes [A]B[A]_{\mathcal{B}}[A]B​.

Interpretation¶

A linear map is fixed by its values on a basis, so the nnn columns [Avj]C[A\boldsymbol{v}_j]_{\mathcal{C}}[Avj​]C​ carry all the information about AAA. The matrix turns the abstract map into arithmetic on coordinates: [Av]C=[A]C,B [v]B[A\boldsymbol{v}]_{\mathcal{C}}=[A]_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B}}\,[\boldsymbol{v}]_{\mathcal{B}}[Av]C​=[A]C,B​[v]B​ for every v∈V\boldsymbol{v}\in Vv∈V.

The figure uses the differentiation example below, with B=(1,t,t2)\mathcal{B}=(1,t,t^2)B=(1,t,t2) and C=(1,t)\mathcal{C}=(1,t)C=(1,t). Going right along the top applies the map DDD itself; going down takes coordinates. Both paths from ppp to (b,2c)⊤(b,2c)^{\top}(b,2c)⊤ agree: differentiating and then taking coordinates gives the same vector as taking coordinates and then multiplying by [D][D][D].

The matrix depends on the map and on both chosen bases, including their order. A frequent mistake is to put the coordinates of AvjA\boldsymbol{v}_jAvj​ in rows instead of columns, or to write them in the standard basis when C\mathcal{C}C is a different basis. For a map Rn→Rm\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^mRn→Rm with the standard bases the representation is the standard matrix from Math II.

The idea matters for eigenvalue problems, which are basis-free questions about a map: choosing a basis of eigenvectors makes the representing matrix diagonal, which is the content of diagonalization.

Key Results¶

  • For fixed bases, A↦[A]C,BA\mapsto[A]_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B}}A↦[A]C,B​ is a bijective linear map from L(V,W)\mathcal{L}(V,W)L(V,W) onto the m×nm\times nm×n matrices; hence dim⁡L(V,W)=mn\dim\mathcal{L}(V,W)=mndimL(V,W)=mn.
  • Composition becomes matrix multiplication: for B ⁣:U→VB\colon U\to VB:U→V and basis D\mathcal{D}D of UUU, [AB]C,D=[A]C,B[B]B,D[AB]_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}}=[A]_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B}}[B]_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{D}}[AB]C,D​=[A]C,B​[B]B,D​ (see product of linear maps).
  • For the identity map on VVV and two ordered bases B,C\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}B,C of VVV, [I]C,B[I]_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B}}[I]C,B​ equals the identity matrix InI_nIn​ if and only if C=B\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}C=B; otherwise it converts B\mathcal{B}B-coordinates into C\mathcal{C}C-coordinates (see change of basis).
  • If PPP is the invertible matrix whose columns are the B\mathcal{B}B-coordinates of a second basis B′\mathcal{B}'B′ of VVV, then [A]B′=P−1[A]BP[A]_{\mathcal{B}'}=P^{-1}[A]_{\mathcal{B}}P[A]B′​=P−1[A]B​P. Representations of one operator are therefore similar and share determinant, trace and eigenvalues.
  • An operator on VVV is invertible if and only if one (equivalently every) representing matrix is invertible.
