Routing Matrix¶

Definition¶

In a network of stations 1,…,M1,\ldots,M1,…,M, Markov routing means that a job finishing service at station iii moves to station jjj with a probability Pij∈[0,1]P_{ij}\in[0,1]Pij​∈[0,1] that does not depend on anything else: not on the job's earlier route, its service times, or the state of the network. The M×MM\times MM×M matrix P=(Pij)\boldsymbol{P}=(P_{ij})P=(Pij​) is the routing matrix, with row iii for the station a job leaves and column jjj for the station it goes to next. The exit probability

Pi0=1−∑j=1MPijP_{i0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{M}P_{ij}Pi0​=1−j=1∑M​Pij​

is the probability that a job leaves the network after service at station iii. Hence every row of P\boldsymbol{P}P has non-negative entries and sums to at most 111; diagonal entries Pii>0P_{ii}>0Pii​>0 model jobs that return to the same station.

Interpretation¶

The routing matrix records where jobs go, separately from how long they take (the service times) and where they come from (the external arrival rates γi\gamma_iγi​). Each row is a conditional probability distribution of the next destination, given the station just left, with the missing mass Pi0P_{i0}Pi0​ assigned to leaving.

Each arrow in the figure is one entry of P\boldsymbol{P}P from Example 1. The arrows leaving station 1 carry P11=P12=1/2P_{11}=P_{12}=1/2P11​=P12​=1/2, so row 1 sums to 111 and nobody leaves from station 1. Station 2 sends 1/41/41/4 back to station 1, 1/41/41/4 to itself, and the remaining P20=1/2P_{20}=1/2P20​=1/2 out of the network, so row 2 sums to 1/21/21/2. The green values λ1=3\lambda_1=3λ1​=3 and λ2=2\lambda_2=2λ2​=2 are the resulting total arrival rates.

Whatever the service-time distributions, the jobs sent from station iii to station jjj form a stream of rate λiPij\lambda_iP_{ij}λi​Pij​; in the notes' setting of Poisson streams this is a thinned Poisson process (Poisson splitting). Station jjj receives the merge of these streams and its external input. This bookkeeping gives the traffic equations λj=γj+∑iλiPij\displaystyle \lambda_j=\gamma_j+\sum_i\lambda_iP_{ij}λj​=γj​+i∑​λi​Pij​, or λ=γ+λP\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\boldsymbol{P}λ=γ+λP with row vectors and the matrix product. They have a sensible solution only if every job eventually leaves: if the rows of the stations it can reach all sum to 111, jobs circulate for ever and the rates blow up (Example 3).

A common slip is the orientation. Rows sum to at most 111, not columns, and the inflow to station jjj uses column jjj, ∑iλiPij\displaystyle \sum_i\lambda_iP_{ij}i∑​λi​Pij​.

  • Pij≥0P_{ij}\ge0Pij​≥0 and ∑j=1MPij≤1\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^MP_{ij}\le1j=1∑M​Pij​≤1 for every iii, with exit probability Pi0=1−∑jPij\displaystyle P_{i0}=1-\sum_jP_{ij}Pi0​=1−j∑​Pij​.
  • Flow on an arc: jobs move from station iii to station jjj at rate λiPij\lambda_iP_{ij}λi​Pij​, and leave the network from station iii at rate λiPi0\lambda_iP_{i0}λi​Pi0​.
  • Traffic equations (7.3.1): λi=γi+∑j=1MλjPji\displaystyle \lambda_i=\gamma_i+\sum_{j=1}^M\lambda_jP_{ji}λi​=γi​+j=1∑M​λj​Pji​, that is, λ=γ+λP\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\boldsymbol{P}λ=γ+λP (Traffic Equations); in a stable network the total departure rate equals ∣γ∣=∑iγi\displaystyle |\boldsymbol{\gamma}|=\sum_i\gamma_i∣γ∣=i∑​γi​.
  • Visit ratios: λi/∣γ∣\lambda_i/|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|λi​/∣γ∣ is the mean number of visits per job to station iii (Visit Ratio).
  • Tandem line: Pi,i+1=1P_{i,i+1}=1Pi,i+1​=1 for i<Mi<Mi<M and PM0=1P_{M0}=1PM0​=1 (Tandem Queue); this P\boldsymbol{P}P satisfies PM=0\boldsymbol{P}^M=\boldsymbol{0}PM=0, because every job has left after MMM steps (Exercise 7.4.1, optional reading).
  • Closed networks: no job leaves, so every row sums to 111 (Closed Queueing Network).
