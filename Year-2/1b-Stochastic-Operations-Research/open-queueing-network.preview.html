Open Queueing Network¶

Definition¶

An open queueing network consists of MMM stations between which jobs move. External jobs, that is, new jobs that have not visited any station yet, arrive at station iii with rate γi≥0\gamma_i\geq0γi​≥0, with ∣γ∣=∑i=1Mγi>0\displaystyle |\boldsymbol{\gamma}|=\sum_{i=1}^M\gamma_i>0∣γ∣=i=1∑M​γi​>0. After service at station iii a job moves to station jjj with probability PijP_{ij}Pij​ or leaves the network with probability Pi0=1−∑j=1MPij\displaystyle P_{i0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^MP_{ij}Pi0​=1−j=1∑M​Pij​, collected in the routing matrix P\boldsymbol{P}P; every job eventually leaves. The state at time ttt is the vector (L1(t),…,LM(t))(L_1(t),\ldots,L_M(t))(L1​(t),…,LM​(t)) of the numbers of jobs at the stations, including those in service.

The model of Section 7.3 adds three assumptions: external jobs arrive as Poisson processes, service times are exponential at every station, and routing is Markov, that is, independent of everything else. Each station is then an M/M/1M/M/1M/M/1 station (or M/M/cM/M/cM/M/c with ccc servers), and the network is often called a Jackson network. In a closed network, by contrast, no job enters or leaves.

Interpretation¶

An open network is the multi-station version of a single queue: work enters from outside, is processed in several steps, possibly with returns, and leaves. The external rates γi\gamma_iγi​ are given; the network turns them, through its routing, into the arrival rate λi\lambda_iλi​ at each station.

The figure is the clinic of Example 1: blue arrows are external arrivals, grey arrows carry routing probabilities, the red arrow is the exit. Because of the return arrow from station 3 to station 1, the green station rates λ=(4,7/3,5)\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(4,7/3,5)λ=(4,7/3,5) exceed the external rates: they follow from the traffic equations, and the exit rate 0.8⋅5=40.8\cdot5=40.8⋅5=4 equals ∣γ∣=3+1|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|=3+1∣γ∣=3+1.

Stability needs two things. Jobs must be able to leave, otherwise the traffic equations have no solution, and every station must have load ρi=λiE[Si]<1\rho_i=\lambda_i\mathbb{E}[S_i]<1ρi​=λi​E[Si​]<1. Comparing γi\gamma_iγi​ with the capacity of station iii is not enough, because fed-back and routed jobs add to the load (Example 3). The Poisson and exponential assumptions are not needed for rates and Little's law; they make each station behave as an M/M/1M/M/1M/M/1 queue with arrival rate λi\lambda_iλi​. The key fact is that the departures of a stationary M/M/1M/M/1M/M/1 queue again form a Poisson process with rate λ\lambdaλ (Exercise 7.3.2), so in a network without feedback the split and merged streams downstream are Poisson too. For general service times the notes treat only tandem lines, with the approximations of Section 7.1.

A common slip is to apply Little's law to the network with a station rate λi\lambda_iλi​ instead of the network rate ∣γ∣|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|∣γ∣.
