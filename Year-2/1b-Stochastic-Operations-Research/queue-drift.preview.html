Queue Drift¶

Definition¶

Consider the discrete-time queue recursion with arrivals aka_kak​ and capacities ckc_kck​ per period, and write

λ=E[ak],μ=E[ck],\lambda=\mathbb{E}[a_k],\qquad\mu=\mathbb{E}[c_k],λ=E[ak​],μ=E[ck​],

the same for every period. The drift of the queue is λ−μ\lambda-\muλ−μ, the expected change of the queue length per period while the queue is long enough for the full capacity to be used. Indeed, whenever Lk−1+ak≥ckL_{k-1}+a_k\ge c_kLk−1​+ak​≥ck​ (service in the arrival period) or Lk−1≥ckL_{k-1}\ge c_kLk−1​≥ck​ (no service in the arrival period), the recursion reduces to

Lk−Lk−1=ak−ck.L_k-L_{k-1}=a_k-c_k.Lk​−Lk−1​=ak​−ck​.

If (ak,ck)(a_k,c_k)(ak​,ck​) is independent of the past and the capacities are bounded, ck≤cˉc_k\le\bar cck​≤cˉ, then E[Lk−Lk−1∣Lk−1=m]=λ−μ\mathbb{E}[L_k-L_{k-1}\mid L_{k-1}=m]=\lambda-\muE[Lk​−Lk−1​∣Lk−1​=m]=λ−μ for every m≥cˉm\ge\bar cm≥cˉ. The queue has positive drift if λ>μ\lambda>\muλ>μ and negative drift if λ<μ\lambda<\muλ<μ.

Interpretation¶

Far from zero a queue moves like a random walk whose step in each period is arrivals minus capacity. The drift is the mean step: its sign says whether a long queue will grow or drain, and its size says how fast.

With ck=7c_k=7ck​=7 the drift is −1-1−1 and the path ends at 303030; with ck=5c_k=5ck​=5 the drift is +1+1+1 and it ends at 505050. The dashed lines 40−k40-k40−k and 40+k40+k40+k are what the drift alone predicts.

The identity Lk−Lk−1=ak−ckL_k-L_{k-1}=a_k-c_kLk​−Lk−1​=ak​−ck​ only holds away from the boundary. Near zero the server cannot serve jobs that are not there, so the queue is pushed up, and a queue with negative drift does not reach a negative length but keeps fluctuating near the origin. Negative drift means λ/μ<1\lambda/\mu<1λ/μ<1, which is the load condition ρ<1\rho<1ρ<1 of (2.1.9). The case λ=μ\lambda=\muλ=μ is not enough: the notes point out that stochastic queues with zero drift are not stable.

Drift sets the direction, not the size of the queue. With the same negative drift a queue can be long if capacity comes in lumps, which is why Section 2.3 next introduces the squared coefficient of variation. With i.i.d. steps, linearity of expectation and the law of large numbers explain why a queue with positive drift grows roughly like n(λ−μ)n(\lambda-\mu)n(λ−μ). In Week 3 this idea returns as rate stability.

Key Results¶

  • Away from the boundary the increment is exactly ak−cka_k-c_kak​−ck​; on average the queue changes by λ−μ\lambda-\muλ−μ per period.
  • Under both timing conventions Lk≥Lk−1+ak−ckL_k\ge L_{k-1}+a_k-c_kLk​≥Lk−1​+ak​−ck​, hence Ln≥L0+∑k=1n(ak−ck)\displaystyle L_n\ge L_0+\sum_{k=1}^n(a_k-c_k)Ln​≥L0​+k=1∑n​(ak​−ck​); with i.i.d. steps and λ>μ\lambda>\muλ>μ the queue grows at least linearly.
  • λ>μ\lambda>\muλ>μ: unstable, the queue grows by about λ−μ\lambda-\muλ−μ per period. λ<μ\lambda<\muλ<μ: a long queue drains by about μ−λ\mu-\lambdaμ−λ per period and then stays near zero. λ=μ\lambda=\muλ=μ: not stable for stochastic queues (Solution 2.3.1).
  • The sign of the drift matches the load estimate ρ≈∑kak/∑kck\displaystyle \rho\approx\sum_ka_k/\sum_kc_kρ≈k∑​ak​/k∑​ck​ compared with 111.
  • A small negative drift gives long and variable queues, and so does variable capacity at the same drift (Examples 3 and 4).
