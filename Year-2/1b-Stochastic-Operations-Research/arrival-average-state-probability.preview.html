Arrival-Average State Probability¶

Definition¶

Let L(t)L(t)L(t) be the number of jobs in the system at time ttt, let AkA_kAk​ be the arrival time of job kkk, and let A(t)A(t)A(t) be the number of arrivals in [0,t][0,t][0,t]. Write L(Ak−)L(A_k-)L(Ak​−) for the left limit of LLL at AkA_kAk​, the number of jobs that job kkk finds just before it enters. The number of arrivals up to time ttt that find nnn jobs is

A(n,t)=∑k=1A(t)1{L(Ak−)=n}.A(n,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{A(t)}\mathbf{1}\{L(A_k-)=n\}.A(n,t)=k=1∑A(t)​1{L(Ak​−)=n}.

provided the limit exists; the two forms agree when A(t)→∞A(t)\to\inftyA(t)→∞. It is the long-run fraction of arriving jobs that see nnn jobs in the system.

Interpretation¶

π(n)\pi(n)π(n) is what customers experience: ask every arriving job how many others it found, and record the fraction of answers equal to nnn. It is a sample average over jobs, whereas p(n)p(n)p(n) is an average over time.

The figure uses the path of Example 1. Each arrow below the time axis is an arrival, and the green dot marks the level just before the jump, which is what that job sees. Two of the four arrivals see 000 and two see 111, so the green bars are 1/21/21/2, 1/21/21/2 and 000. The grey outlines are the time fractions 0.20.20.2, 0.50.50.5 and 0.30.30.3 of the same path: the customers saw a much emptier system than the server did.

The left limit matters. Because LLL jumps up at AkA_kAk​, the value L(Ak)L(A_k)L(Ak​) already counts the arriving job; using it shifts every observation up by one. The two averages differ because arrival times are not spread evenly over time: in Example 2 every job arrives just after the previous job has left. A common slip is to estimate π(n)\pi(n)π(n) by time sampling, or p(n)p(n)p(n) by asking customers. For Poisson arrivals, built by the arrival-time recursion with exponential gaps, PASTA in Week 4 shows that the two averages coincide.
