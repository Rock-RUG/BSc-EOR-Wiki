Stationary-Probability Recursion¶

Method¶

Let L(t)L(t)L(t) be the number of jobs in a queueing or inventory system in which jobs arrive and depart one at a time. Assume that the long-run limits defining the time-average probabilities p(n)p(n)p(n), the state-dependent arrival rates λ(n)\lambda(n)λ(n) and the departure rates μ(n+1)\mu(n+1)μ(n+1) (the rate at which jobs leave while n+1n+1n+1 are present) exist. When arrivals can be blocked, λ(n)\lambda(n)λ(n) is replaced everywhere by the entry rate λ+(n)\lambda^+(n)λ+(n). Given λ(n)\lambda(n)λ(n) for n≥0n\geq0n≥0 and μ(n)>0\mu(n)>0μ(n)>0 for n≥1n\geq1n≥1, the method has three steps.

Step 1, recursion. The level-crossing equation λ(n)p(n)=μ(n+1)p(n+1)\lambda(n)p(n)=\mu(n+1)p(n+1)λ(n)p(n)=μ(n+1)p(n+1) gives

p(n+1)=λ(n)μ(n+1) p(n),n≥0,p(n+1)=\frac{\lambda(n)}{\mu(n+1)}\,p(n),\qquad n\geq0,p(n+1)=μ(n+1)λ(n)​p(n),n≥0,

and therefore

p(n+1)=λ(n)λ(n−1)⋯λ(0)μ(n+1)μ(n)⋯μ(1) p(0).p(n+1)=\frac{\lambda(n)\lambda(n-1)\cdots\lambda(0)}{\mu(n+1)\mu(n)\cdots\mu(1)}\,p(0).p(n+1)=μ(n+1)μ(n)⋯μ(1)λ(n)λ(n−1)⋯λ(0)​p(0).

Interpretation¶

The recursion turns local information, how fast the system moves up from nnn and down from n+1n+1n+1, into the whole stationary distribution. Each step across one level multiplies by the ratio of the upward to the downward rate; only p(0)p(0)p(0) is unknown, and the requirement that the probabilities add up to one fixes it.

The figure is the pizza stand of Example 1. Above each gap stands the arrival rate λ(n)\lambda(n)λ(n), below it the service rate μ(n+1)\mu(n+1)μ(n+1), and their ratio is the factor that carries the weight q(n)q(n)q(n) to q(n+1)q(n+1)q(n+1). The weights 1,13,118,…1,\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{1}{18},\dots1,31​,181​,… add up to G≈1.414G\approx1.414G≈1.414, and dividing by GGG gives the green row p(n)p(n)p(n), which starts at p(0)=1/G≈0.707p(0)=1/G\approx0.707p(0)=1/G≈0.707.

Three conditions matter. Jobs must move one at a time, because otherwise a level can be crossed by a jump that skips it and the level-crossing equation gains extra terms (the batch-service queue in balance equations is an example). The ratio uses μ(n+1)\mu(n+1)μ(n+1), not μ(n)\mu(n)μ(n), since the departure that crosses the level between nnn and n+1n+1n+1 starts in state n+1n+1n+1. Finally, the normalizing sum must be finite: when it diverges, the long-run fraction of time in every state is zero and there is no stationary distribution. A constant ratio ρ\rhoρ turns the sum into a geometric series, which is finite only for ρ<1\rho<1ρ<1.

A common slip is to use the arrival rate λ(n)\lambda(n)λ(n) in a state where arrivals are blocked; only jobs that enter move the system up, so λ+(n)\lambda^+(n)λ+(n) belongs in the recursion.

3. The recursion in Python. The function below implements the three steps; with the rates of Example 1 it reproduces the probabilities above.

def stationary_probs(lam, mu, n_max):
    q = [1.0]  # unnormalized weights, q(0) = 1
    for n in range(n_max):
        q.append(q[-1] * lam(n) / mu(n + 1))
    G = sum(q)  # G = 1/p(0)
    return [x / G for x in q]


lam = lambda n: [10, 10, 20, 20, 5][n] if n < 5 else 0
mu = lambda n: 30 * min(n, 2)
p = stationary_probs(lam, mu, 5)
print([round(x, 4) for x in p])  # [0.7072, 0.2357, 0.0393, 0.0131, 0.0044, 0.0004]

For a queue without blocking, n_max is a truncation level that must be raised until the results no longer change. The oven case of Section 5.2 uses the same idea as a check: raising the capacity from K=10K=10K=10 to K=1000K=1000K=1000 recovers the M/M/1M/M/1M/M/1 results.
