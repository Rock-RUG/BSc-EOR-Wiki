Queueing and Inventory Systems¶

Definition¶

A queueing system is a system to which jobs (customers, patients, orders) arrive over time, wait while the service capacity is occupied, are served and then leave. An inventory system is a system in which items are produced or ordered in advance and stored until demand takes them; demand that finds no stock is either lost or has to wait for a replenishment.

Both are described by stochastic processes such as the number of jobs L(t)L(t)L(t) in the system at time ttt or the stock on the shelf. For queueing systems the course uses four key performance measures:

  1. the fraction of time p(n)p(n)p(n) that the system contains nnn jobs, so p(0)p(0)p(0) is the fraction of time the system is empty and the server idle;
  2. the fraction of jobs π(n)\pi(n)π(n) that find nnn jobs in the system upon arrival;
  3. the mean, variance or distribution of the waiting time;
  4. the mean, variance or distribution of the number of jobs in the system.

Inventory systems have analogous measures: the average time items spend on the shelf, the fraction of demand served from stock, the fraction of lost sales and the fraction of items scrapped.

Interpretation¶

A queue stores jobs until capacity is free; a stock stores items until demand arrives. The two are the same picture seen from opposite sides, which is why the course treats them together.

In the upper row arrivals A(t)A(t)A(t) join the queue and leave after service as departures D(t)D(t)D(t); the job count L(t)L(t)L(t) is what p(n)p(n)p(n) and π(n)\pi(n)π(n) describe. In the lower row the shelf is replenished and emptied by demand. The dashed arrow is the change of perspective: an item on the shelf is a "job in queue", and its "service time" is the time until the next customer who buys one, as in Example 2.

Each measure answers a different question. The time fractions p(n)p(n)p(n) describe the system as the server or the manager sees it, and π(n)\pi(n)π(n) describes what customers see; the two need not agree. Control is always a balance: more capacity or more stock shortens waits and lost sales, but costs idle servers, holding cost or scrapped items. The same trade-off between holding and shortage appeared in the inventory policies of OR Modelling.

Real systems seldom have closed-form answers, so the course first builds sample paths that a computer can simulate, starting with the arrival-time recursion, and only then derives exact and approximate models.

Key Results¶

  • Jobs in a queueing system and demand in an inventory system play the role of arrivals; a hamburger on the shelf corresponds to a job in queue, with the time until the next buying customer as its "service time".
  • Time-average state probabilities p(n)p(n)p(n) take the server's perspective, and arrival-average state probabilities π(n)\pi(n)π(n) the customer's perspective.
  • Inventory performance is measured by shelf time, the fraction of demand met from stock, the fraction of lost sales and the scrap rate.
  • In Week 2 the same view returns in discrete time: the discrete-time queue recursion for queues and the single-item inventory system, where negative inventory levels are backlogged demand.

Examples¶

1. Hospital workload. A hospital department follows about 100010001000 patients who each visit twice a year, and a working year has 200200200 days. The department therefore sees on average 1000⋅2/200=101000\cdot2/200=101000⋅2/200=10 patients per working day. With 101010 minutes per consultation this is 10⋅10=10010\cdot10=10010⋅10=100 minutes of consultation work per day, the amount of capacity the physicians must supply on an average day. The question for the planner is how to schedule so that urgent patients are seen within five working days without leaving physicians idle.

2. Hamburgers on a shelf. A restaurant prepares hamburgers at minute 000; each can be sold for 888 minutes and is then scrapped. On day 1 customers arrive at minutes 222, 555 and 777; on day 2 at minutes 222 and 666.

  • Stocking 333 hamburgers: on day 1 all three customers are served, and the hamburgers spend 222, 555 and 777 minutes on the shelf, on average 14/3≈4.714/3\approx4.714/3≈4.7 minutes. On day 2 two are sold and one is scrapped at minute 888. Over both days: no lost sales, 111 of 666 hamburgers scrapped.
  • Stocking 222 hamburgers: on day 1 the customer at minute 777 finds an empty shelf, and on day 2 both are sold. Over both days: 111 of 555 customers lost, nothing scrapped.

Neither stock level avoids both lost sales and scrap once demand varies from day to day; choosing the level is a control problem. Seen from the hamburgers, they form a queue that is "served" by arriving customers: on day 1 the shelf times 222, 555 and 777 play the role of sojourn times.
