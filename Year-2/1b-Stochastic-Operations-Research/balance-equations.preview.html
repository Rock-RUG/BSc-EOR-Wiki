Balance Equations¶

Proposition¶

Let L(t)L(t)L(t) be the number of jobs in a system in which jobs arrive and depart one at a time, and assume the limits defining p(n)p(n)p(n), the state-dependent arrival rates λ(n)\lambda(n)λ(n) and the departure rates μ(n)\mu(n)μ(n) exist; here μ(n)\mu(n)μ(n) is the rate at which jobs leave while nnn are present. Let

I(n,t)=A(n−1,t)+D(n,t),O(n,t)=A(n,t)+D(n−1,t),I(n,t)=A(n-1,t)+D(n,t),\qquad O(n,t)=A(n,t)+D(n-1,t),I(n,t)=A(n−1,t)+D(n,t),O(n,t)=A(n,t)+D(n−1,t),

where A(n,t)A(n,t)A(n,t) counts the arrivals in [0,t][0,t][0,t] that find nnn jobs and D(n,t)D(n,t)D(n,t) the departures that leave nnn jobs behind. Thus I(n,t)I(n,t)I(n,t) counts how often LLL enters state nnn and O(n,t)O(n,t)O(n,t) how often it leaves state nnn. Since entries and exits of one state alternate, ∣I(n,t)−O(n,t)∣≤1|I(n,t)-O(n,t)|\leq1∣I(n,t)−O(n,t)∣≤1, and dividing by ttt and letting t→∞t\to\inftyt→∞ gives, for every n≥1n\geq1n≥1, the balance equation

λ(n−1)p(n−1)+μ(n+1)p(n+1)=(λ(n)+μ(n))p(n).\lambda(n-1)p(n-1)+\mu(n+1)p(n+1)=\bigl(\lambda(n)+\mu(n)\bigr)p(n).λ(n−1)p(n−1)+μ(n+1)p(n+1)=(λ(n)+μ(n))p(n).

For state 000 it reads μ(1)p(1)=λ(0)p(0)\mu(1)p(1)=\lambda(0)p(0)μ(1)p(1)=λ(0)p(0). When arrivals can be blocked, λ(n)\lambda(n)λ(n) is replaced by the entry rate λ+(n)\lambda^+(n)λ+(n).

Interpretation¶

Draw a box around one state and count how often the sample path crosses the box boundary. Every entry into the state is followed by an exit, so in the long run the rate of flow into the state equals the rate of flow out. A balance equation is this statement written with rates times time fractions.

The figure is the batch-service queue of Example 2, with λ=1\lambda=1λ=1 and μ=2\mu=2μ=2. State 1 is entered by an arrival from state 0 and by a batch completion from state 3, and it is left only by an arrival, because the server waits until two jobs are present. The flows across the box are λp(0)+μp(3)=413+213\lambda p(0)+\mu p(3)=\tfrac{4}{13}+\tfrac{2}{13}λp(0)+μp(3)=134​+132​ in and λp(1)=613\lambda p(1)=\tfrac{6}{13}λp(1)=136​ out.

Level crossing cuts the state space into two parts by a line between two neighbouring states, and such a cut does not always exist, for instance when the state is a vector. A box around one state needs no cut, which is why balance equations carry over to such systems, networks of stations among them. In a birth-death queue nothing new is gained: the balance equation of state nnn is the sum of the level-crossing equations of levels n−1n-1n−1 and nnn.

Boundary states need their own equation, because some transitions are missing there. A common slip is to read μ(n)\mu(n)μ(n) as 1/E[S]1/\mathbb{E}[S]1/E[S] in every model. It is a long-run rate measured on the sample path: exponential service times give μ(n)=μ\mu(n)=\muμ(n)=μ by the memoryless property, but in the M/D/1M/D/1M/D/1 queue the departure rates μ(n)\mu(n)μ(n) are in general not equal to 1/E[S]1/\mathbb{E}[S]1/E[S] (True/False 4.6.4 of the notes).

Key Results¶

  • For single-unit transitions and n≥1n\geq1n≥1: λ(n−1)p(n−1)+μ(n+1)p(n+1)=(λ(n)+μ(n))p(n)\lambda(n-1)p(n-1)+\mu(n+1)p(n+1)=(\lambda(n)+\mu(n))p(n)λ(n−1)p(n−1)+μ(n+1)p(n+1)=(λ(n)+μ(n))p(n) (equation 4.6.11 of the notes); state 000 gives λ(0)p(0)=μ(1)p(1)\lambda(0)p(0)=\mu(1)p(1)λ(0)p(0)=μ(1)p(1).
  • The counting argument does not need single-unit jumps: for every state, the long-run rate at which the state is left equals the rate at which it is entered. Each term is a transition rate out of a state times the fraction of time in that state.
  • In a birth-death queue the balance equations are equivalent to the level-crossing equations λ(n)p(n)=μ(n+1)p(n+1)\lambda(n)p(n)=\mu(n+1)p(n+1)λ(n)p(n)=μ(n+1)p(n+1), so the stationary-probability recursion solves them.
  • With blocking at capacity KKK, the boundary equation is λ+(K−1)p(K−1)=μ(K)p(K)\lambda^+(K-1)p(K-1)=\mu(K)p(K)λ+(K−1)p(K−1)=μ(K)p(K) because λ+(K)=0\lambda^+(K)=0λ+(K)=0.
  • Together with normalization, the balance equations determine p(n)p(n)p(n); they are the tool for queueing networks, where a single cut between two levels does not exist.
