Server Adjustment¶

Definition¶

A server adjustment is a short task, such as replacing a blunt knife or making an unexpected phone call, that the server must carry out between two jobs at a moment that cannot be planned. It is a non-preemptive outage: it never interrupts a job in service.

The course model: after job iii an adjustment is needed when Fi=1F_i=1Fi​=1, where Fi∼Bern⁡(p)F_i\sim\operatorname{Bern}(p)Fi​∼Bern(p) with 0<p<10<p<10<p<1, so P(Fi=1)=p\mathbb{P}(F_i=1)=pP(Fi​=1)=p. The adjustment takes time RiR_iRi​, with mean E[R]\mathbb{E}[R]E[R] and variance Var⁡(R)\operatorname{Var}(R)Var(R). The net service times S0,iS_{0,i}S0,i​, the adjustment times RiR_iRi​ and the indicators FiF_iFi​ each form an i.i.d. sequence, and the three sequences are independent of each other. Job iii then occupies the server for the effective service time

The number BBB of jobs served between two consecutive adjustments counts the trials up to and including the next success, so BBB has the first success distribution FS⁡(p)\operatorname{FS}(p)FS(p) with E[B]=1/p\mathbb{E}[B]=1/pE[B]=1/p. The notes call BBB geometric; on this site that name is reserved for the count of failures.

Interpretation¶

An adjustment is a server setup whose timing is left to chance. With setups the server processes exactly BBB jobs per outage; with adjustments the count between outages is random, while the long-run fraction of outages per job is the same p=1/E[B]p=1/\mathbb{E}[B]p=1/E[B].

Both rows of the figure contain nine jobs and three outages RRR, so the average number of jobs per outage is 333 in each. In the top row a setup precedes every batch of B=3B=3B=3 jobs. In the bottom row each job is followed by an adjustment with probability p=1/3p=1/3p=1/3, and this sample path gives B=3B=3B=3, 555 and 111: the outages are as frequent, but irregularly spaced.

Hence the mean effective service time is the same as for setups, but its variance is larger. Conditioning on FFF and applying Adam's law and Eve's law gives an extra term p(1−p)(E[R])2p(1-p)(\mathbb{E}[R])^2p(1−p)(E[R])2, which is the variance of the Bernoulli switch FFF scaled by the mean adjustment. For E[R]>0\mathbb{E}[R]>0E[R]>0 it is positive whenever 0<p<10<p<10<p<1; it would vanish only in the excluded extremes p∈{0,1}p\in\{0,1\}p∈{0,1}, when nothing is random about whether an adjustment follows. The independence assumptions matter: if adjustments became more likely after long jobs, FFF and S0S_0S0​ would be correlated and the variance formula would need a covariance term.

Adjustments do not change the arrivals, so λ\lambdaλ and Ca2C_a^2Ca2​ stay as they are, and Sakasegawa's formula only needs E[S]\mathbb{E}[S]E[S] and Cs2=Var⁡(S)/(E[S])2C_s^2=\operatorname{Var}(S)/(\mathbb{E}[S])^2Cs2​=Var(S)/(E[S])2. A common slip is to compare policies by variability alone: planning adjustments removes the extra term, but if planning makes the outages more frequent, the higher load can cost far more (Example 3).
