Arrival Theorem¶

Theorem¶

Consider a closed network with n≥1n\geq1n≥1 circulating jobs, mmm single-server first-come-first-served stations with exponential service times of mean E[Si]\mathbb{E}[S_i]E[Si​], and fixed or Markov routing. When a job arrives at station iii, the other n−1n-1n−1 jobs it sees are distributed over the stations according to the stationary distribution of the same network with n−1n-1n−1 jobs. In particular, the mean number of jobs it finds at station iii is E[Li(n−1)]\mathbb{E}[L_i(n-1)]E[Li​(n−1)], and its mean time at station iii per visit is

E[Ji(n)]=(1+E[Li(n−1)])E[Si],n≥1.\mathbb{E}[J_i(n)]=\bigl(1+\mathbb{E}[L_i(n-1)]\bigr)\mathbb{E}[S_i],\qquad n\geq1.E[Ji​(n)]=(1+E[Li​(n−1)])E[Si​],n≥1.

Interpretation¶

A job on its way to a station does not see itself. It sees the other n−1n-1n−1 jobs, and the theorem says that they look exactly like a network in equilibrium with one job fewer. The question "what does an arriving job find?" thus reduces to the time-average behaviour of a smaller network.

The wide blue bars are the time-average probabilities p3(k)=(1,2,4,8)/15p_3(k)=(1,2,4,8)/15p3​(k)=(1,2,4,8)/15 of kkk jobs at station 1. The narrow green bars are the arrival-average probabilities seen by jobs arriving at station 1: (1,2,4,0)/7(1,2,4,0)/7(1,2,4,0)/7, the time-average law p2(k)p_2(k)p2​(k) of the network with two jobs. An arrival never finds all three jobs at station 1, because it is one of them.

The theorem is not PASTA: the arrivals at a station of a closed network are not a Poisson process, and what they see differs from the time average. The theorem holds for the product-form closed network described here. For the formula for E[Ji(n)]\mathbb{E}[J_i(n)]E[Ji​(n)] one more fact is needed: by the memoryless property, the job in service when our job arrives still needs on average E[Si]\mathbb{E}[S_i]E[Si​], so each of the jobs found, and the job itself, contributes one mean service time. With general service times the remaining service time of the job in service would have a different mean.

The common slip is to use E[Li(n)]\mathbb{E}[L_i(n)]E[Li​(n)] instead of E[Li(n−1)]\mathbb{E}[L_i(n-1)]E[Li​(n−1)]; it lets the arriving job queue behind itself (Example 3). The theorem is the engine of mean value analysis.

Key Results¶

  • Mean time per visit. E[Ji(n)]=(1+E[Li(n−1)])E[Si]\mathbb{E}[J_i(n)]=\bigl(1+\mathbb{E}[L_i(n-1)]\bigr)\mathbb{E}[S_i]E[Ji​(n)]=(1+E[Li​(n−1)])E[Si​] (equation (7.5.1)): wait for the E[Li(n−1)]\mathbb{E}[L_i(n-1)]E[Li​(n−1)] jobs found, then receive the own service.
  • One job. For n=1n=1n=1 the job always finds an empty network, so E[Ji(1)]=E[Si]\mathbb{E}[J_i(1)]=\mathbb{E}[S_i]E[Ji​(1)]=E[Si​].
  • Arrival average versus time average. The law seen by arrivals in the nnn-job network is the time-average law of the (n−1)(n-1)(n−1)-job network, not that of the nnn-job network.
  • Population recursion. Together with Little's law, E[Li(n)]=ViTH1(n)E[Ji(n)]\mathbb{E}[L_i(n)]=V_i\mathrm{TH}_1(n)\mathbb{E}[J_i(n)]E[Li​(n)]=Vi​TH1​(n)E[Ji​(n)] with the visit ratios ViV_iVi​, the theorem links population nnn to population n−1n-1n−1.
